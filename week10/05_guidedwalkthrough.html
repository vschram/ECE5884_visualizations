Week 11 · Diversity: Receive Combining, Transmit Diversity, and the Alamouti Code

Follow the Week 11 sequence: examine how large reductions in received signal power (deep fades) motivate independent diversity branches and distinguish array gain from diversity gain. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) measures signal power relative to noise power; an outage occurs when SNR is below the chosen threshold. Combine Nr receive branches using selection combining (SC), equal-gain combining (EGC), and maximal-ratio combining (MRC); explain why fixed-weight repetition from several transmit antennas provides no diversity; and use the Alamouti code to obtain diversity order 2 without channel-state information at the transmitter (CSIT).

Motivation, diversity types, and array gain versus diversity gain

Independent branches reduce the probability of simultaneous deep fades

With one antenna, |h|² is exponentially distributed: a branch can experience a deep fade even when its average SNR is high. Average SNR alone therefore does not describe link reliability. Diversity provides several independently fading observations of the signal. Combining them raises the mean SNR (array gain) and reduces the probability of very low SNR more rapidly as the average SNR increases (diversity gain). Diversity gain requires sufficiently independent fading branches. MRC still provides array gain when the branches fade together. Spatial correlation depends on antenna spacing, angular spread, antenna patterns, and mutual coupling.

        SIMO receive diversity: selection, equal-gain, and maximal-ratio combining

        SC selects the strongest branch, EGC aligns all branch phases, and MRC applies channel-dependent amplitude and phase weights

        A single-input multiple-output (SIMO) receiver with Nr antennas observes y = √G h s + v and forms z = wHy. Selection combining (SC) retains the strongest branch. Equal-gain combining (EGC) aligns the phases and applies equal magnitudes. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, maximal-ratio combining (MRC), with w ∝ h, maximizes the output SNR and gives γMRC = Σγi. For independent, identically distributed Rayleigh branches, SC and MRC both achieve diversity order Nr, but MRC has the larger array gain. The receiver must estimate h; the least-squares (LS) option demonstrates the effect of estimation error.

                  MISO transmit diversity: repetition, delay diversity, and maximum-ratio transmission

                  Fixed-weight repetition produces one effective channel, whereas delay diversity and channel-aware transmission preserve independent channel contributions

                  In a multiple-input single-output (MISO) link with Mt transmit antennas, the transmitted signals combine in the propagation channel before reaching the single receive antenna. Without channel-state information at the transmitter (CSIT), equal-power repetition gives heff = Σhk/√Mt. For independent, zero-mean, unit-variance circularly symmetric complex Gaussian channel coefficients, denoted CN(0,1), heff is also CN(0,1), so repetition retains diversity order 1. Delay diversity creates a frequency-selective single-input single-output (SISO) channel whose path energies can be collected by equalization and coding. With CSIT, maximum-ratio transmission (MRT), f = h*/‖h‖, aligns the channel contributions at the receiver.

                        The Alamouti space-time code

                        Conjugation and a sign change create an orthogonal channel matrix, enabling diversity order 2 and linear detection without CSIT

                        The two antennas transmit (s[0], s[1])/√2 in the first slot and (−s*[1], s*[0])/√2 in the second while h1 and h2 remain constant. Stacking y[0] and y*[1] gives ỹ = √(G/2) H s + ṽ, where HHH = (|h1|² + |h2|²) I and HH denotes the conjugate transpose. Multiplying by HH, the matched-filter operation, removes interference between the two symbols, and each symbol has SNR GExα/(2N0). This is 3.01 dB below MRT, which requires CSIT.

                              Review questions
                              1. Define array gain and diversity order. How many branches give order 4 with MRC?

                              Array gain is the increase in average SNR, Ag = γ̄Σ/γ̄. Diversity order M is the high-SNR slope, P̄s ≈ cγ̄−M: M decades per 10 dB. MRC over Nr independent, identically distributed branches has order Nr, so four independent branches are needed. Check this result on the outage plot in Tab 2.

                              2. Derive the MRC weights for y = √G h s + v.

                              The SNR of z = wHy is |wHh|²GEx/(‖w‖²N0). By Cauchy–Schwarz, |wHh|² ≤ ‖w‖²‖h‖², with equality for w ∝ h. Hence γMRC = GEx‖h‖²/N0 = Σγi: the dashed circle on the complex-vector panel of Tab 2.

                              3. Why does sending s/√2 from both antennas provide no diversity?

                              The receiver observes heff = (h1 + h2)/√2. For independent, identically distributed CN(0,1) channels, heff is again CN(0,1), which has the same distribution as a SISO channel. The diversity order therefore remains 1, and the two channel contributions can cancel. See the complex-vector panel and repetition lab in Tab 3.

                              4. Show that HHH is diagonal for the Alamouti code.

                              With H = [h1 h2; h2* −h1*], the off-diagonal entry is h1*h2 + h2(−h1*) = 0, and both diagonal entries are |h1|² + |h2|².

                              5. Explain the 3 dB gap between Alamouti and MRT.

                              Both received SNRs depend on ‖h‖², so both schemes have diversity order 2. Without CSIT, Alamouti divides Ex equally between the antennas and cannot direct the transmitted signal toward the receiver, giving GExα/(2N0). MRT uses CSIT to align the channel contributions and gives GExα/N0, which is twice the received SNR in every fading block.
