1. Geometry

The detour through the selected point creates an excess path Δ.

2. Phase

The extra distance produces a phase difference of 2πΔ/λ. An additional phase φ₀ may also be present.

3. Superposition

The two fields add. In-phase fields reinforce; out-of-phase fields partly or completely cancel.

A full 360° phase cycle returns the fields to the same alignment. Between the listed values, the interference changes gradually. For φ₀=0, at Δ=λ/4 the fields are 90° apart and the interference cross term is zero.

Why the radius changes

A point away from the line of sight creates a longer Tx–point–Rx path. The nth boundary occurs when this extra distance is nλ/2. The radius is largest near the midpoint, increases with wavelength, and therefore becomes smaller at higher carrier frequencies.

Constructive and destructive interference

When Δ=mλ, the fields arrive in phase and reinforce each other. When Δ=(m+1/2)λ, they arrive 180° apart and oppose each other. Destructive interference gives complete cancellation only when the two field amplitudes are equal.

What the Fresnel zones mean

Within each zone, the propagation phase changes continuously by 180°. Contributions from neighbouring zones therefore tend to oppose one another and partly cancel. An obstacle can disturb this balance and often reduce the received field.

Practical clearance

A common line-of-sight design guideline is to maintain obstacle clearance of at least about 0.6r₁. This is a practical clearance rule, not a separate Fresnel boundary.

Suggested student exploration: Compare Δ=0 with Δ=λ/2. Then select the 180° additional phase shift and observe that the constructive and destructive cases swap. Finally, reduce α at Δ=λ/2 to see why unequal amplitudes do not cancel completely.

The diagram uses exact geometric boundaries, but the vertical dimension is enlarged automatically for visibility. The interference calculation is a simplified narrowband two-field model: the fields are assumed coherent and compatibly polarised. Real links may also include diffraction, several reflected paths, path loss, and frequency-selective effects.
